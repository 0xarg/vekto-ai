Middleware modernization is rarely a technology decision. It starts when a
vendor announces an end-of-support date, a license renewal arrives at a number
nobody wants to sign, or the last engineer who understood the estate leaves.

The problem is that the estate is load-bearing. Every integration on it is
carrying live traffic for something the business depends on, and nobody has a
complete picture of what depends on what.

## What the program involves

The sequence matters more than the tooling. Attempting to redesign while
migrating is the most common way these programs fail — it turns one hard
problem into two, and makes it impossible to tell whether a fault came from the
move or the redesign.

1. **Establish what actually exists.** Not what the documentation says. The
   inventory and the dependency graph come first, and usually contain surprises.
2. **Move behavior before changing it.** Get onto the target platform with
   behavior preserved, then improve from a position where you can compare
   against something that works.
3. **Retire deliberately.** The old platform stays available until the new one
   has run the same load for long enough to trust it.

## What changes for your team

Your engineers end up owning implementations on a platform with a living talent
market, in source control, reviewable through the same process as the rest of
your code. That is a different operating model from a proprietary ESB where
change requests queue behind a small number of specialists.

## What this does not solve

Modernization does not fix integration designs that were wrong to begin with. A
poorly bounded integration moved onto a modern platform is a poorly bounded
integration with better tooling around it. Redesign is a separate program, and
it is easier once the first one is finished.
